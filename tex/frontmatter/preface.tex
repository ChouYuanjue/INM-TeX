
\chapter*{Preface}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Preface}

Mathematics rarely arrives in the order in which textbooks present it. A concept is often met first at its edge: as a trick in an exercise, a diagram on a blackboard, a sentence in a lecture, or an unexpected resemblance between two calculations. Only later does the surrounding structure become visible. A theorem that was once merely something to apply acquires a proof; a proof reveals a language; that language returns and makes the original problem look different. This book grew out of many such returns.

The notes collected here were written over a long period and for no single purpose. Some began in the middle of solving a problem. Others followed a lecture, a conversation, a reading, an examination, or an attempt to explain an idea clearly enough that I could no longer hide from what I did not understand. None of them was written with a future table of contents in mind. Brought together, they do not trace a straight road from elementary mathematics to advanced mathematics. They record something less orderly and, I think, more faithful: the repeated encounter with the same questions under changing forms.

One of the pleasures of keeping old mathematical notes is discovering how much later theory was already present in an early calculation, though not yet named. The Euclidean algorithm can be learned as a procedure for finding a greatest common divisor, and later recognized as a statement about ideals. A determinant begins as a formula and becomes a measure of volume, orientation, and the action of a linear map on an exterior power. A line integral becomes the pullback of a differential form; a Fourier coefficient becomes a coordinate in a Hilbert space. These later descriptions are not ornaments placed on elementary work to make it appear more profound. At their best, they explain why the elementary calculation had the shape it did, and why it continues to matter.

The reverse movement is equally important. Abstraction is most useful when it can return to examples without condescension. A general definition should make a familiar computation clearer, not merely rename it. For that reason, the notes retain many explicit calculations, diagrams, special cases, failed approaches, and local arguments. They are often the places where a structure becomes visible before it becomes formal. The aim of the reorganization has been to strengthen those passages---to correct errors, supply missing arguments, and clarify transitions---without replacing the texture of discovery with the false smoothness of hindsight.

This also means allowing a note to remain situated in its own time. A seminar, a book, a conversation, or even a passing image may matter because it determined which question came into view. Such circumstances belong in the mathematics when they explain the direction of thought; they do not belong there merely as autobiography. I have therefore tried to preserve the moments in which an idea first became compelling, as well as the doubts that naturally arose from it, while avoiding the voice of a later editor standing over an earlier self and announcing what should already have been understood.

The resulting book is neither a textbook nor a diary. It is an archive made readable. Some chapters are expository, some exploratory, and some return to material that has appeared before from a different angle. Repetition is sometimes unavoidable, but it is not always redundant: the same object can be elementary in one context, structural in another, and still mysterious in a third. The order of the book offers one way through this material, not a hierarchy of importance and not a prescribed course of study. It is entirely reasonable to enter at a question, follow its references outward, and leave by a route that the surrounding arrangement did not anticipate.

The word \emph{informal} in the title is not meant as permission for carelessness. Definitions should be accurate, arguments should be checkable, and examples should carry real mathematical weight. It means instead that the notes remain close to the conditions under which understanding is actually formed: partially, recursively, and through use. They do not claim to settle the subjects they touch. Their purpose is more modest and more durable---to preserve enough of the path that an idea can be found again, examined more closely, and carried further.

A notebook remains valuable because it does not have to have the last word. It can keep a theorem beside the problem that made it necessary, an abstraction beside the calculation that gave it meaning, and an unanswered question beside the best explanation presently available. This book is an attempt to give that kind of continuity a stable form.
