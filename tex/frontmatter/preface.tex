\chapter*{Preface}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Preface}

One of my early notes contains a table of dualities that, at the time, I could barely understand. It matches Stone spaces with Boolean algebras, affine schemes with rings, and, further down, topoi with logoi. Keyao Peng shared it at Geek College. The first row was already strange enough: for a Stone space, knowing only the algebra of its subsets that are both open and closed is enough to reconstruct the points and topology. I wanted to know how that could be true, and what on earth the remaining rows meant.

A geometry problem in these pages may need only a diagram and a good observation. A question about sets or spaces may take several definitions before reaching its answer. There is algebra and topology, analysis and probability, and later notes on numerical methods and machine learning. Some pages grew out of talks and conversations; others answered problems from school. The chapters therefore differ in length and in what they expect a reader to know.

The earliest notes gave me an excuse to practice \LaTeX. I was learning set theory at the time, and spent an unreasonable amount of time making the equations look right. A friend's talk on category theory helped draw me further into foundations. I also translated Tom Leinster's \emph{Rethinking Set Theory}; corresponding with him encouraged me to keep reading. As school became busier, elementary mathematics and competition problems found their way into the collection as well.

The collection gradually spread beyond TeX files. Handwriting was faster, and much of the later material lived in Flexcil, in book margins, or on pages assembled from earlier notes. Some pieces began in blog posts or correspondence. Written in Chinese, English, and occasionally Russian, they could finally be brought together with the help of tools for reading handwriting and mixed-format documents. I have gathered them into an English TeX edition, revisiting explanations and diagrams along the way. The layout is inspired by Evan Chen's \emph{An Infinitely Large Napkin}, which taught me a great deal and influenced the appearance of my first typed notes.

I have kept the dates on the chapters and grouped the notes by subject. There is no need to read them in order. If a question or a picture catches your eye somewhere in the middle, that is a perfectly good place to begin.
